# Antipatterns — Antagonist

Problems with the antagonist that undermine the story's central conflict.

---

## Diffused Villainy

Multiple antagonists watering down the evil. Reader can't focus resistance, fear, or hatred on one clear target.

**Test:** Count the primary antagonistic forces. If there's more than one character driving the conflict at full strength, consider collapsing them into one.

**Fix:** Consolidate. One clear villain creates sharper conflict than a committee of mean people.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Morally Shallow (Merely Naughty)

The antagonist is inconvenient but not morally bad. They have no genuine depravity — just unpleasantness.

**Test:** Do they have a cause they believe in, or just preferences? Are they capable of genuine harm, or just friction?

**Why it matters:** Readers sense the difference. A character who's just annoying creates a story that feels low-stakes.

**Fix:** Morally bad doesn't mean moustache-twirling evil. The antagonist needs a worldview that collides with the protagonist's in a way that can't be reasoned around. A genuine conviction, even if wrong.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Cartoon Villain

The antagonist who exists only to be antithetical. No charm, no enticement, no humanity.

**Why it matters:** Even villains need something that draws people to them. Without it, the antagonist becomes a punchline. "The mustachioed antagonists of yesteryear only provoke laughs today."

**Test:** What does the antagonist offer that might make a reader halfway sympathize? Are they funny? Do they believe in something? Is there a version of their argument that almost makes sense?

**Fix:** Give the villain a human entry point. They don't need to be likable — but they need to be understandable from the inside. A grievance that reads as legitimate from a certain angle. The best antagonists are ones you could almost root for if you squinted.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Villain Bio Trap

The writer models the villain on a real enemy — someone they genuinely resent or dislike. Emotional proximity prevents craft.

**Why it matters:** The writer can't give the villain appeal because doing so feels like a betrayal of who they're punishing in the story.

**Test:** Did the villain's model come from someone the writer actively dislikes or has a grievance with? Is the villain given any traits that would make them recognizable to their real counterpart?

**Fix:** Use composites, not portraits. Pull from multiple sources — core quality from one, mannerisms from another, backstory from a third. Or flip: write the villain as someone the real person would never recognize. Distance is the tool.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Humanizing Technique

**The problem:** You can't make the villain charming on your own — your own feelings are in the way.

**The fix:** See the villain through the eyes of someone who loves or cares about them. A follower, a spouse, an admirer. Write a scene from that character's perspective. The villain's appeal becomes visible when filtered through genuine care.

**Why it works:** Caring reveals texture. The lover sees the charm. The follower sees the cause. The rival sees the intelligence. That texture feeds back into the villain's main presence.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing
