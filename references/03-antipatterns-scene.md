# Antipatterns — Scene

Scene-level problems that drain energy, momentum, and reader investment.

---

## The Info Dump

Large blocks of background information delivered upfront. History, context, explanation — dumped before the story starts.

**Why it fails:** Readers can only absorb so much before their eyes glaze over. And there's nothing to hook them in.

**Fix:** Distribute information across scenes where it's relevant. Deliver context through conflict, not explanation.

**Sources:** Gotham Writers

---

## False Tension

Tension that doesn't have real stakes underneath. Characters are upset, situations look dire — but nothing actually matters.

**Why it fails:** Readers sense when conflict is theatrical rather than genuine. False tension creates fatigue.

**Fix:** Ensure the stakes are personal and specific. The reader needs to believe the character actually has something to lose.

**Sources:** Gotham Writers

---

## The Boring Sentence

Sentences that don't earn their place. They exist to transmit information but don't do anything with it — no rhythm, no image, no pressure.

**Fix:** Every sentence should either advance the story or deepen the moment. If it does neither, tighten or cut.

**Sources:** Gotham Writers

---

## Summary Voice

When the narration sounds like a summary of events rather than immediate experience. Things happened; the prose describes them from a distance.

**Why it fails:** The reader experiences events as report rather than experience — no tension, no stakes, no scene.

**Fix:** Get specific. Find the concrete image, the physical detail, the moment in real time that carries the meaning. Render scenes, don't summarize them.

**Sources:** Gotham Writers

---

## Static Characters (Scene Level)

Characters who remain unchanged by what happens to them within a scene. The scene happens to them but they don't absorb or react to it.

**Why it fails:** Events wash over the character without changing their state. The scene has no internal journey.

**Fix:** Track the character's internal state from opening to close. What shifts? What breaks? What does the event cost them?

**Sources:** Gotham Writers

---

## No Visual Texture

Two or more consecutive pages without a visual element — a concrete image, a physical detail, a visible action.

**Why it fails:** Fiction is visual. Abstract summary for more than a page breaks the reader's footing in the scene.

**Fix:** Replace narrative summary with an immediate scene — or compress it to a single image. Make sure something can be seen on every page.

**Sources:** Gotham Writers

---

## Weak Scene Opening

Scenes that open with setup instead of momentum. The writer is still establishing context before the story starts.

**What a scene opening should do:**
1. Establish character (who is in this scene)
2. Create forward momentum (what is happening or about to happen)

**Fix:** Get into the scene later. Start with action or a complication — not explanation.

**Sources:** Gotham Writers

---

## Dead-End Scene Ending

Scenes that end with summary or explanation rather than action or revelation.

**Why it fails:** The scene ends without propelling the reader forward. It feels like a dead end instead of a bridge.

**Fix:** End with momentum — a change in situation, a revelation, a decision, or a question the reader needs answered.

**Sources:** Gotham Writers

---

## Between-the-Scenes Flatness

Offstage recounting of actions not seen. Passages that bridge scenes — "Meanwhile, back at..." material, retrospectives, explaining what happened.

**Why it fails:** The writer is explaining what happened rather than showing it. The reader experiences it as summary.

**Fix:** Cut entirely if the event doesn't need to be shown. Dramaticize if it does — make it a scene rendered in real time. Or compress to a single image that lands with force.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing
