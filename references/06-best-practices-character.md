# Best Practices — Character

Proven techniques for building characters that linger in the reader's mind.

---

## Give Them a Vice Palette

Not a single quirk — a range of specific, slightly embarrassing behaviors that are distinctly theirs.

**Why it works:** Texture creates memorability. A character with distinctive habits is harder to replace with a different name in a scene.

**The standard:** Can you describe their goals but not their friction? Could a scene be rewritten with a different name and lose nothing? If yes, the character lacks texture.

**Source:** Stein on Writing

---

## The Protagonist Grill

A stress-test to surface how well the writer actually knows their protagonist.

**How to run:** Ask the writer questions about the protagonist one at a time. Don't move on until there's a real answer — not "I'll figure it out later."

**Question categories:**

1. **Surface facts** — What do they look like? What do they own? What's their daily routine?
2. **Obsessions and avoidances** — What can't they stop doing? What do they refuse to touch?
3. **Friction in scenes** — In a tense moment, what do they do that they can't control?
4. **Blind spots** — What do they believe about themselves that's wrong?
5. **Values under pressure** — When forced to choose between safety and truth, what do they reach for first?
6. **Voice** — Finish this sentence: *"I can't stand it when people —"*
7. **Unfinished business** — What did they want at the start? What do they want at the midpoint? What do they actually need by the end?

**Verdict:** If any category produces "I don't know" — flag it. That's a gap.

**Source:** Stein on Writing

---

## Distinctive Trait as Anchor

Give the character a distinctive trait — positive or negative — that the writer is certain they don't have.

**Why it works:** A trait the writer doesn't share creates genuine distance between author and character. The character becomes their own person.

**The standard:** Something specific, observable, slightly embarrassing or idiosyncratic.

**Source:** Stein on Writing

---

## The Collision Must Land

Transformation must be structural, not cosmetic.

**Why it works:** Novel-length fiction makes an implicit promise: this person will be different on page 300 than they were on page 1. When that promise goes unkept, the reader feels robbed.

**The standard:** Something in the story needs to hit the protagonist hard enough that they can't stay who they were. The change can be positive, negative, or ambiguous — but it must be visible and structural.

**Ask:** What happens at the midpoint that forces them to choose between who they were and who they need to become?

**Source:** Stein on Writing

---

## One-Thing Minor Characters

One specific characteristic is enough for a minor character.

**Why it works:** The reader's imagination fills in the rest. The trick is picking the right one — not a quirk, but a behavior that implies an interior.

**The standard:** A gesture, a mannerism, a vocal pattern, a way of entering a room. Something that suggests what the person wants, fears, or would never say out loud.

**The failure:** Set dressing instead of person. A throwaway character who's clearly a prop breaks the fictional dream faster than a plot hole.

**Source:** Stein on Writing
