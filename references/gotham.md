# Gotham Writers Workshop — Writing Fiction

Primary source: *Writing Fiction* by Gotham Writers Workshop. A craft guide covering scene, dialogue, voice, point of view, narrative structure, revision, and more.

---

## Scene Craft

### What Makes a Scene

A scene is a story unit in which something happens — someone acts or speaks, with stakes and tension driving the story forward. Scenes have their own engine; if you strip away the exposition, the scene should still have a reason to exist as a dramatic unit.

**Scene vs. Summary:**
- Scene: rendered in real time, showing events as they happen
- Summary: condenses time, tells readers what happened rather than showing it

Good fiction moves between scene and summary fluidly. Too many scenes back-to-back can feel breathless; too much summary loses the reader.

---

### Scene Opening

Every scene needs an opening line that does at least two things:
1. Establishes character (who is in this scene)
2. Creates forward momentum (what is happening or about to happen)

The opening should pull the reader into the scene immediately — not set up context before the story starts.

---

### Scene Ending

Scenes should end with momentum — either a change in situation, a revelation, a decision, or a question the reader needs answered. A scene that ends without propelling the reader forward feels like a dead end.

**Common failure:** Scenes that end with summary or explanation rather than action or revelation.

## Dialogue

### What Dialogue Must Do

Dialogue is not transcription — it's compression with purpose. Every line of dialogue should either:
1. Reveal character
2. Advance the plot
3. Create tension or conflict

If a line does none of these, cut it.

---

### The Subtext Rule

Great dialogue almost never says what it means directly. There's typically a gap between what's said and what's meant. The reader senses the gap and leans in.

**Failure mode:** Characters say exactly what they mean. The dialogue becomes informational rather than dramatic.

**Fix:** For each line of dialogue, ask — what's the character really saying? What are they hiding, avoiding, or misdirecting?

---

### Character Distinctiveness

Each character should have a recognizable voice — not just in accent or speech pattern, but in how they think and what they care about. A line of dialogue should be identifiable to its speaker without attribution.

**Diagnosis:** Can you tell who's speaking if you cover the dialogue tags? If not, the voices aren't distinct enough.

---

### Power in Dialogue

Dialogue is a site of negotiation, resistance, and hidden agendas. Characters in conversation are rarely just exchanging information — they're trying to get something, defend something, or hide something.

**Tip:** Give each character awant in the conversation. Something they're working toward or protecting.

## Voice

### Voice as Fingerprint

Voice is the sum of every choice a writer makes — word selection, sentence rhythm, the things noticed and the things skipped, the register and temperature of the narration. It's not style on top of content; it's inseparable from content.

**Key distinction:** The narrator's voice and the author's voice are not the same. The author can write in many voices. The narrator is a constructed persona with specific perception and presence.

---

### First Person Narration

First person creates intimacy and immediacy, but also limits. The reader only knows what the narrator knows, feels, and observes.

**Challenge:** The narrator's voice must be distinctive, consistent, and specific enough to sustain a whole novel. Generic first person ("I walked to the store and saw something strange") has no fingerprint.

**Pitfall:** First person as author mouthpiece — the narrator sounds like the author instead of a person.

---

### Third Person

Third person allows the narrator to enter multiple characters' perspectives while maintaining some narrative distance.

**Close third:** The narrator enters one character's consciousness at a time, creating intimacy while allowing authorial control.

**Omniscient:** The narrator knows everything — can enter any mind, comment on events, shift perspective freely. Demands a strong, compelling authorial voice to carry the distance.

---

### Point of View Consistency

Choose a point of view and stay consistent within a scene. Switching mid-scene creates disorientation unless the switch itself is a deliberate effect.

**Rule:** If you start a scene in one character's head, the reader expects to stay there until a scene break signals a change.

## Narrative Structure

### The Scene Chain

Stories are made of scenes linked by cause and effect. One scene ends with a question or complication that propels the reader into the next.

**The test:** Remove any scene and ask what breaks. If nothing breaks, the scene isn't doing enough structural work.

---

### The Promise of the Premise

Every story makes an implicit promise to the reader about what kind of experience it will deliver. The narrative should fulfill that promise.

**Example:** A thriller promises escalating danger and resolution of the central mystery. If it delivers character study instead, the reader feels cheated.

**Fix:** Be clear in the opening what kind of story this is. Then deliver on that contract.

---

### Endings

Endings must do two things:
1. Resolve the central dramatic question (or deliberately refuse to)
2. Create a shift in the reader's understanding — what they thought the story was about vs. what it was actually about

**Failure modes:**
- Sudden twist that isn't set up — feels arbitrary
- Resolved too neatly — leaves no resonance
- Ambiguous just to be ambiguous — the uncertainty has no weight

**Good endings** feel both inevitable and surprising. The reader looks back and sees it could only have ended this way — but didn't see it coming.

## Revision

### The Gap

After finishing a draft, put it down. Let it cool. Come back with fresh eyes.

Distance lets you see what's actually on the page rather than what you intended to write.

---

### Scene-by-Scene Audit

Go through the manuscript scene by scene. For each scene, ask:
- What is this scene doing?
- Does it earn its place?
- Could it be cut with no loss?
- If kept, what is the one change that would make it stronger?

---

### Voice Revision

First pass: tighten prose — cut unnecessary words, sentences, paragraphs. Remove what doesn't serve the story.

Second pass: read aloud. Where you stumble, the prose stumbles. Dialogue that's hard to speak aloud doesn't sound like speech.

Third pass: consistency of voice. Does the narrator feel like one person throughout? Do all characters sound distinct?

## Antipatrons (Gotham Framework)

*These are problems Gotham identifies in fiction — antipatterns that break the fictional dream.*

### The Info Dump

Large blocks of background information delivered upfront. History, context, explanation — dumped before the story starts.

**Why it fails:** Readers can only absorb so much before their eyes glaze over. And there's nothing to hook them in.

**Fix:** Distribute information across scenes where it's relevant. Deliver context through conflict, not explanation.

---

### False Tension

Tension that doesn't have real stakes underneath. Characters are upset, situations look dire — but nothing actually matters.

**Why it fails:** Readers sense when conflict is theatrical rather than genuine. False tension creates fatigue.

**Fix:** Ensure the stakes are personal and specific. The reader needs to believe the character actually has something to lose.

---

### The Boring Sentence

Sentences that don't earn their place. They exist to transmit information but don't do anything with it — no rhythm, no image, no pressure.

**Fix:** Every sentence should either advance the story or deepen the moment. If it does neither, tighten or cut.

---

### Static Characters

Characters who remain unchanged by what happens to them. They may be interesting, sympathetic, well-written — but they don't absorb the story's events into themselves.

**Note:** Short stories can be exempt. A window can show someone exactly as they are without requiring transformation. Novels make an implicit promise of change.

---

### Summary Voice

When the narration sounds like a summary of events rather than immediate experience. Things happened; the prose describes them from a distance.

**Fix:** Get specific. Find the concrete image, the physical detail, the moment in real time that carries the meaning. Render scenes, don't summarize them.
