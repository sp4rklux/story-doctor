# Antipatterns — Dialogue

Dialogue problems that break the fictional dream or drain conversation energy.

---

## Loaded Dialogue Tags

Dialogue tags that tell the reader how to feel instead of letting them feel it. "Muttered," "screamed," "hissed," "whispered," "snapped," "crooned."

**The problem:** The dialogue should carry how it's spoken, not the tag. If the line reads like it's being screamed, the reader hears it as screamed.

**Fix:** Replace loaded tags with "said." If the line doesn't convey the intended delivery without the tag, rewrite the line itself — the dialogue should do the work, not the attribution.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Dialogue That's Informational

Characters saying exactly what they mean. Dialogue that exists only to transmit information rather than create dramatic tension.

**Why it fails:** Great dialogue almost never says what it means directly. There's typically a gap between what's said and what's meant. The reader senses the gap and leans in.

**Fix:** For each line of dialogue, ask — what's the character really saying? What are they hiding, avoiding, or misdirecting?

**The subtext rule:** The reader should feel the tension between what's said and what's meant.

**Sources:** Gotham Writers

---

## Indistinct Voices

Characters who sound interchangeable. Cover the dialogue tags and you can't tell who's speaking.

**Why it fails:** Each character should have a recognizable voice — not just in accent or speech pattern, but in how they think and what they care about.

**Fix:** Give each character a want in every conversation. Something they're working toward or protecting. Voice emerges from what the character notices, what they avoid, what they care about.

**Test:** Can you tell who's speaking without attribution? If not, the voices aren't distinct enough.

**Sources:** Gotham Writers

---

## Too-Polite Dialogue

Dialogue that's too cooperative, too easily resolved, or too nice.

**Why it fails:** Dialogue is a site of negotiation, resistance, and hidden agendas. If characters always agree, defer, or resolve cleanly, there's no pulse.

**Fix:** Push characters against each other. Give each one something they're defending.

**Sources:** Gotham Writers

---

## Oblique Responses as Default

Characters who never answer directly — or conversely, characters who always answer directly when oblique would create more tension.

**The trap:** Predictable response patterns flatten dialogue. Always oblique feels evasive. Always direct feels flat.

**Fix:** Mix it up. Real people do both. The key is knowing which serves the scene — sometimes the direct answer lands harder precisely because it's unexpected.

**Sources:** Gotham Writers

---

## Monologue Dialogue

Long uninterrupted speeches — three or more sentences without interjection from another character.

**Why it fails:** Length without interruption feels like a monologue, not a conversation. Real speech is interrupted, trailed, half-finished.

**Fix:** Break up long dialogue with an interjection — a thought, an action, a look, another character's response.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Complete Sentences Only

Characters who always speak in grammatically complete sentences.

**Why it fails:** Real speech is fragmented. People interrupt themselves, trail off, speak in half-thoughts.

**Fix:** Fragment, don't complete. Let characters trail, interrupt, leave sentences unfinished.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Flat Exchange

One dialogue exchange that stands out as weaker than the rest of the conversation.

**Why it fails:** A single flat exchange breaks the fictional dream in the middle of an otherwise alive conversation. The reader's pattern-recognition fires: fake.

**Fix:** Compare dialogue exchanges. If one seems weak or wrong compared to others, mark it for improvement or cutting.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing
