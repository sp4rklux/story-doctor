# Best Practices — Plot & Structure

Proven techniques for building plots that move and structures that hold.

---

## The Scene Chain

Stories are made of scenes linked by cause and effect. One scene ends with a question or complication that propels the reader into the next.

**The test:** Remove any scene and ask what breaks. If nothing breaks, the scene isn't doing enough structural work.

**The standard:** Every scene should create a question the next scene answers — or raise a new one.

**Source:** Gotham Writers

---

## The Promise of the Premise

Every story makes an implicit promise about what kind of experience it will deliver.

**Why it works:** The reader enters with expectations. The narrative should fulfill the promise — or deliberately subvert it with purpose.

**Example:** A thriller promises escalating danger and resolution of the central mystery. Deliver character study instead and the reader feels cheated.

**The standard:** Be clear in the opening what kind of story this is. Then deliver on that contract.

**Source:** Gotham Writers

---

## Endings That Feel Inevitable and Surprising

Good endings feel both inevitable and surprising at the same time.

**Why it works:** The reader looks back and sees it could only have ended this way — but didn't see it coming.

**Failure modes:**
- Sudden twist that isn't set up — feels arbitrary
- Resolved too neatly — leaves no resonance
- Ambiguous just to be ambiguous — the uncertainty has no weight

**The standard:** The reader should be able to trace the path backward and find every clue. But the path forward should have felt mysterious.

**Source:** Gotham Writers

---

## Escalating Stress

Fiction deals with the most stressful moments of characters' lives.

**Why it works:** If the pressure never builds, the story has no engine. The reader commits when the stakes commit.

**Two questions:**
1. Are your characters under stress from time to time?
2. Does the stress increase?

**The standard:** The midpoint should raise the stakes. The climax should demand everything. If stress plateaus, the story plateaus.

**The test:** Track stress across three acts — opening, midpoint, climax. Signal red if stress doesn't meaningfully increase from opening to climax.

**Source:** Stein on Writing

---

## The Midpoint Commits the Story

The midpoint is where the story commits — where the pressure must build or the whole thing sags.

**Why it works:** Everything before the midpoint is setup. Everything after is fallout. If the pressure doesn't build at the midpoint, it won't build anywhere.

**Ask:** What happens here that raises the cost? What does the character now have to face that they weren't facing before?

**Source:** Stein on Writing

---

## Real Conflict — The Antagonist Earns Their Role

The antagonist must be the right obstacle — not just powerful, but unavoideable.

**Why it works:** If the protagonist could achieve their goal regardless of who opposes them, the conflict isn't real.

**The test:** Strip the antagonist's identity away. Does the plot collapse without them?

**The standard:** The thing standing between the protagonist and what they want must be something that can't be reasoned with, bargained with, or skipped.

**Source:** Stein on Writing

---

## The First Scene Creates an Itch

The opening scene must create a question, not finish an explanation.

**Why it works:** If the reader understands everything by the end of scene one, there's nowhere left to go.

**What "compelling" looks like:**
- A question the reader needs answered
- A character in motion toward something they want
- A voice that pulls you in
- A situation that feels unstable

**What "no compelling reason" looks like:**
- Status quo the reader has no reason to care about
- Nothing at stake yet
- Writer still setting up instead of starting
- Character calm and comfortable

**Source:** Stein on Writing

---

## Scene Ends With Momentum

Scenes should end with momentum — not dead ends.

**Why it works:** A scene that ends without propelling the reader forward feels like a stop sign, not a bridge.

**The standard:** End with a change in situation, a revelation, a decision, or a question the reader needs answered.

**Source:** Gotham Writers

---

## The Gap — Distance Heals

After finishing a draft, put it down. Let it cool. Come back with fresh eyes.

**Why it works:** Distance lets you see what's actually on the page rather than what you intended to write.

**The standard:** The writer's memory of what they meant and what's actually on the page are two different documents.

**Source:** Gotham Writers
