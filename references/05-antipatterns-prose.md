# Antipatterns — Prose

Prose-level problems that tax the reader, break immersion, or signal amateur execution.

---

## Purple Prose

Writing that is overblown, overwrought attempts at emotion.

**Examples:**
- *"The cry of a soul in torment, swept by a tide of anger and outrage"*
- *"Terror plucked at her taut nerves"*
- *"Jagged laughter tore at her throat"*
- *"Fierce rending triumph"*

**Root cause:** Sentimentality — an excess of response to a stimulus. The writer is performing emotion rather than creating it in the reader.

**Why it fails:** One bad piece of purple prose can break the fictional dream for the entire manuscript. The reader feels the heavy hand.

**Fix:** Strip back. Replace the overblown phrase with the specific, concrete image that earns the emotion. The reader will feel it without being performed at.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Monotone Rhythm

All sentences approximately the same length. The prose becomes a flatline — readable but uneventful.

**Why it fails:** Variation in sentence length creates momentum and emphasis. Uniform rhythm puts the reader to sleep.

**The mellifluous trap:** A naturally flowing cadence is lovely for a few sentences — then it becomes soporific if kept up unbroken.

**Fix:** Vary sentence length deliberately. Follow a long sentence with a short, even abrupt one. Let the short sentence land like a window slam.

**The trap:** Don't overcorrect. Short-long-short-long can get almost as monotonous as all long or all short. The variation should feel natural, not metronomic.

**Test:** Read aloud. Where you stumble, the rhythm stumbles.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Repeated Words

The same word — common or uncommon — appearing twice within a few pages.

**Why it fails:** Repetition of an uncommon word sticks out like a broken note. Repetition of a common word can feel careless.

**Fix:** Watch for repeated words, especially distinctive ones. Reach for a synonym.

**Exception:** Repetition of a common functional word (the, and, a) is normal. The issue is repetition of words that carry weight or distinctiveness.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Redundant Writing

Two phrases conveying the same idea, or saying the same thing twice in different words.

**Why it fails:** Redundancy is dead weight. The reader gets the same information twice and the prose bloats.

**Fix:** Pick the stronger phrase. Cut the other.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Excessive Adjectives and Adverbs

Adjectives and adverbs that don't add meaning the sentence can't live without. Especially "very."

**Why it fails:** Every unnecessary word is a tax on the reader's attention. Modifiers that don't work don't add texture — they dilute it.

**Fix:** Cut unnecessary adjectives and adverbs. If the word doesn't add meaning, it goes. Cut "very" almost always.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Clichés

Borrowed language that arrived without earning its place.

**Why it fails:** Clichés signal the writer checked out. The phrase was already written, so why write it again?

**Fix:** Mark every cliché for exclusion. Rewrite with fresh language — or cut entirely if no fresh version arrives.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Imprecise Words

The word used is not exactly the word needed — approximate words that pass because the writer knows what they meant.

**Why it fails:** The reader doesn't have the writer's context. If the word isn't exact, the sentence loses precision — and sometimes meaning entirely.

**Fix:** Don't settle for close. Consult a dictionary. Consult a thesaurus. The right word is usually not the first synonym that appears.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Word Order Mismatch

The sentence's emphasis falls on the wrong word because of word order.

**The principle:** Word order shapes emphasis. What lands at the end of a sentence carries the most weight. Moving words, phrases, or clauses changes what the reader focuses on.

**Why it fails:** The sentence says something slightly different from what the writer intended. The emphasis lands on the wrong idea.

**Fix:** Read for where the sentence's natural emphasis lands. Transpose until the logic is obvious. In a book-length manuscript, this happens hundreds of times.

**Consequence before cause:** Often confusing. Cause before consequence flows naturally.

**Example:**
> *"If Paul goes to jail, I won't have anywhere. I can't pay the mortgage on my own."*

The reader doesn't know "anywhere what" until the next sentence. Transpose: give the reason first, then the consequence.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Author Voice Bleed

The narrative voice shifts from the story's established perspective to the author's own commentary or presence.

**Why it fails:** The reader is yanked out of the story. The author's presence intrudes where a constructed narrator should be.

**Fix:** Mark for deeper revision. This requires the deeper POV methodology — don't attempt a quick fix in the revision session.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing

---

## Unearned Information

Information that exists because the plot requires it — not because the story earned it.

**Why it fails:** The reader feels the author's hand. Events are conjured, not earned.

**Fix:** Plant earlier. The gun that fires in Act 3 should appear in Act 1. Plant seeds so the payoff feels inevitable rather than convenient.

**Sources:** Stein on Writing
