# Best Practices — Prose & Dialogue

Proven techniques for prose that earns its place and dialogue that pulses.

---

## Concrete Images Over Abstract Summary

Fiction is visual. When the prose goes abstract and summary-heavy, the reader loses their footing.

**Why it works:** A specific image lands harder than a general statement. The reader sees it and feels it without being told how.

**The standard:** Find the concrete image, the physical detail, the moment in real time that carries the meaning. Render scenes, don't summarize them.

**The failure:** Consecutive pages without a visual element. Prose that describes what happened rather than showing it as it happens.

**Source:** Stein on Writing

---

## Dialogue Carries Its Own Weight

Every line of dialogue should earn its place — not just transmit information.

**Why it works:** Dialogue that's only informational flattens the scene. Every line should either reveal character, advance the plot, or create tension.

**The standard:** If a line does none of these, cut it.

**The subtext rule:** Great dialogue almost never says what it means directly. There's typically a gap between what's said and what's meant.

**Source:** Gotham Writers

---

## Each Character Wants Something in Every Conversation

Dialogue is negotiation, resistance, hidden agendas. Characters are rarely just exchanging information.

**Why it works:** When each character has a want, the conversation has engine. Nothing meaningful happens when characters are simply being cooperative.

**The standard:** Give each character something they're working toward or protecting in every conversation.

**Source:** Gotham Writers

---

## The Right Word, Not the Close Word

Imprecise words pass through drafts easily. The writer knows what they meant, so the wrong word feels right.

**Why it works:** Approximate words cost precision — and sometimes meaning entirely. The right word is usually not the first synonym.

**The discipline:** Consult a dictionary. Consult a thesaurus. Look it up until the exact word is found.

**Source:** Stein on Writing

---

## Vary Sentence Length for Effect

Sentence rhythm is a tool, not an accident.

**Why it works:** Variation creates momentum and emphasis. The short sentence that follows a long one lands like a window slam.

**The discipline:** Follow an especially long sentence with a short one. Let the contrast create the beat.

**The trap:** Don't make it metronomic. Short-long-short-long is as monotonous as all short or all long. The variation should feel like a surprise, not a pattern.

**Source:** Stein on Writing

---

## Word Order as Emphasis Tool

Word order shapes what the reader focuses on. The end of a sentence carries the most weight.

**Why it works:** Transposition changes what the sentence says even when the words are the same. The landing word determines the emphasis.

**The discipline:** Read for where the sentence's natural emphasis lands. Ask: does this match what I'm actually trying to say?

**Dialogue attribution:** Put attribution first if the reader might lose track of who's speaking. If clear, attribution can follow or be omitted.

**Source:** Stein on Writing

---

## Every Word Earns Its Place

Every unnecessary word is a tax on the reader's attention.

**Why it works:** Tight prose moves. Bloated prose slows. The reader's attention is finite — spend it only on what matters.

**The standard:**
- Cut unnecessary adjectives and adverbs
- Cut "very"
- Don't say the same thing twice in different words
- Watch for repeated words within a few pages — use a synonym

**Source:** Stein on Writing

---

## Read Aloud

Prose that's hard to speak aloud is hard to read.

**Why it works:** The mouth catches what the eye misses. Where you stumble, the reader stumbles.

**The standard:** Dialogue that's difficult to speak aloud doesn't sound like speech. Passages where rhythm goes flat become invisible on the page but audible in the mouth.

**Source:** Gotham Writers

---

## Plant and Fire — Chekhov's Gun

Plant the gun earlier. When it fires later, the audience saw it coming. They waited for it. The use becomes almost inevitable.

**The bonus:** Plant it and don't fire it when the audience expects — creates different suspense. They watch the gun, wondering when it will go off.

**Why it works:** Unearned events feel conjured. Earned events feel inevitable. The reader should be able to trace the path backward and find every seed.

**The failure mode:** A gun appears in the hands of someone who isn't known to carry one, and is almost immediately fired. The audience feels the author's heavy hand.

**Source:** Stein on Writing

---

## Cut Without Mercy

When in doubt, do not change anything — but do consider cutting.

**Why it works:** Tightening is a separate discipline from rewriting. The goal is excision, not revision.

**The discipline:** If something is questionable, make a note and move on. The goal is forward momentum through the manuscript, not solving every problem in real time.

**The standard:** Flag it, note it, keep moving. Fixes come in a later pass. The manuscript gets a second pass.

**The exception:** Deliberate slow scenes are valid — they create contrast and allow the reader to breathe. Only flag unintentional slowdowns.

**Source:** Stein on Writing
