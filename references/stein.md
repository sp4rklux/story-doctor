## Character Antipatterns

### The Autobiography Problem

The main character sounds too much like the writer. Same voice, same reactions, same vocabulary, same worldview.

**Test:** Ask whether the protagonist would make the same choices the writer would in a values conflict. If yes, flag as seam.

**Fix:** Give the character a distinctive trait — something the writer is certain they don't have. Specific, observable, slightly embarrassing.

---

### Boring Protagonist

The main character is competent, sympathetic, and entirely forgettable.

**Test:** Can you describe their goals but not their friction? Could a scene be rewritten with a different name and lose nothing?

**Fix:** The character needs texture — not a quirk, but a trait that creates friction. Something slightly embarrassing that gets in their way.

---

### Frozen Character (Static Protagonist)

The protagonist ends the novel the same way they began it.

**Exception:** Experimental work like *Waiting for Godot* — stasis as philosophical condition. Otherwise, assume change is mandatory for novels.

**Fix:** Something must hit the protagonist hard enough they can't stay who they were. Fracture, not cosmetic growth.

## Antagonist Problems

### Diffused Villainy

Multiple antagonists watering down the evil. Reader can't focus resistance, fear, or hatred on one clear target.

**Fix:** Consolidate. One clear villain creates sharper conflict than a committee of mean people.

---

### Morally Shallow (Merely Naughty)

The antagonist is inconvenient but not morally bad. They have no genuine depravity — just unpleasantness. Readers sense the difference.

**Fix:** Morally bad doesn't mean moustache-twirling evil. The antagonist needs a worldview that collides with the protagonist's in a way that can't be reasoned around. A genuine conviction, even if wrong.

---

### Cartoon Villain

The antagonist who exists only to be antithetical. No charm, no enticement, no humanity. "The mustachioed antagonists of yesteryear only provoke laughs today."

**Fix:** Give the villain a human entry point. Understandable from the inside. A grievance that reads as legitimate from a certain angle. The best antagonists are ones you could almost root for if you squinted.

---

### Villain Bio Trap

The writer models the villain on a real enemy — someone they genuinely resent. Emotional proximity prevents craft. The writer can't give the villain appeal because it feels like betrayal.

**Fix:** Use composites, not portraits. Pull from multiple sources. Or flip: write the villain as someone the real person would never recognize.

---

### Humanizing Technique

See the villain through the eyes of someone who loves or cares about them. Write a scene from that character's perspective. The villain's appeal becomes visible when filtered through genuine care.

## Scene Evaluation

### Most Memorable Scene Test

Ask the writer directly: *"What is the most memorable scene in your book? Don't go to your manuscript. If you can't remember it, it isn't memorable."*

**What to listen for:**
- Fast answer, specific → Good
- Hesitation → Yellow flag
- "I can't think of one" → Red flag. The story is atmospherically pleasant but dramatically flat.

---

### Least Memorable Scene Test

*"What's the scene you remember least? Go back and browse — don't read word for word. You know it when you see it."*

**Red flags:**
- Writer insists "has to be there for information" — usually means attachment, not necessity
- Writer doesn't recognize it as a scene — probably summary, not scene

---

### Compare the Two

*"What's the difference? One has a pulse, one doesn't. What is the most memorable scene doing that the other isn't?"*

The writer often sees the problem immediately once side by side. The gap is the fix.

---

### Revise or Cut

*"If you can't think of how to make the least memorable scene work, cut it."*

**Exception:** Only if the scene carries genuinely needed information — and even then, find a way to deliver it inside an existing scene with momentum.

## Motivation

### The Rule

Motivation has to be either:
1. **Provoked by circumstance:** Something in the story forces the character to act
2. **Planted ahead of time:** A seed placed earlier so the action feels inevitable

If neither — it's probably authorial convenience.

---

### Chekhov's Gun

*"If someone has a gun in the first act, they must use it by the third act. Amongst playwrights, that is known as an obligatory scene."*

**Failure mode:** Gun appears in the hands of someone who isn't known to carry one, and is almost immediately fired. The event was conjured, not earned.

**Fix:** Plant the gun earlier. When it fires later, the audience saw it coming and waited for it.

**Bonus:** Plant it and then *don't* fire it when expected — creates different suspense. Audience watches the gun, wondering when it will go off.

---

### Three Most Important Actions Test

Ask the writer from memory (no manuscript): *"What are the three most important actions in your novel?"*

Then for each: *"Is this action motivated in a way you would accept if someone else told the story?"*

**Why from memory:** If the writer can't recall their own three biggest actions, the story is running on autopilot — events happening because the plot requires them.

---

### Full Audit

After the three main actions, review any other significant actions. Ferret out anything that happens just because the author wants it to.

**Sequence:**
1. Identify each significant action outside the three big ones
2. Test each: provoked by circumstance, or planted ahead?
3. If neither: find a scene to plant the motive — or cut the action
4. Only then do a full manuscript read-through

**Why this order:** Don't revise a scene until the motivation problem in it is fixed first. You're polishing a house with a cracked foundation.

## Plot — Opening Scene

### Does Scene One Make You Read Scene Two?

After character work and a rest period, start the second pass: plot.

**Test:** Read scene one. Ask: *"Would I go on to read the second scene?"*

**If no:** The opening hasn't sparked curiosity. Review scene craft guidelines to fix it.

**What "compelling reason" looks like:**
- A question the reader needs answered
- A character in motion toward something they want
- A voice that pulls you in
- A situation that feels unstable

**What "no compelling reason" looks like:**
- Status quo the reader has no reason to care about
- Nothing at stake yet
- Writer still setting up instead of starting
- Character calm and comfortable

**The rule:** First scene must create an itch, not finish an explanation.
